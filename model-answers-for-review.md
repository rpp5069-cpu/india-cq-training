## Question 1 — Who Has the Final Say When Engineers Disagree?
*(Slide 52 — Q17, asked by Marcus Gill)*

**Full question:** "When Indian and Japanese engineers disagree about a design change, who should have final approval, and how can that process preserve both safety and trust?"

**[DRAFT — awaiting team review]**

**Drive.** An unresolved design disagreement on a safety-critical system is not a process gap — it is a liability. With Power Distance at 77, a junior engineer's objection may never reach the room (Hofstede et al., 2010). Silence is not consent.

**Knowledge.** In high-PDI cultures, objections surface one-on-one after the meeting, not in front of the group (Meyer, 2014). A team member who interviewed with India-based product development confirmed this: issues went quiet after bad news was shared; more one-on-ones and direct involvement of local leads restored the information flow (Mark, personal communication, November 14, 2026).

**Strategy.** Before the first real dispute arises, write down who decides what. Create a decision-rights matrix naming a single technical authority per domain — agreed by both sides and in writing. Build a safety stop-channel: any engineer at any level can flag a safety concern without rank-based filtering.

**Action.** Collect objections one-on-one before every formal review. Run a brief private check-in with the Indian team lead 24–48 hours before sign-off. If a safety concern surfaces at any stage, it overrides seniority — document this rule in the project charter. Decide who decides before you disagree, and let safety outrank seniority.

*(~210 words)*

## Question 2 — Who Owns a Failure at the Interfaces?
*(Slide 53 — Q18, asked by Will Miller)*

**Full question:** "When it comes to the cooperation and mix of Japanese track design, European signaling, and Indian rolling stock, who would own a failure at those interfaces, and how should the partnership define accountability before the first incident rather than after?"

**[DRAFT — awaiting team review]**

**Drive.** An interface without a named owner is a failure without an owner. The place where Japanese, European, and Indian standards meet is exactly where unspoken assumptions accumulate fastest.

**Knowledge.** Cross-cultural outsourcing partnerships succeed when both sides actively close gaps in expectations and processes (Heeks et al., 2001). With India's uncertainty avoidance at 40, a verbal commitment may genuinely mean "we will try our best" rather than a binding guarantee (Hofstede et al., 2010). Shared boundary objects — joint documentation, written acceptance criteria, overlap hours — are what convert cultural ambiguity into engineering accountability (Krishna et al., 2004).

**Strategy.** Before the first handoff, name one interface owner on each side plus a cross-cultural bridge person who understands both engineering contexts. Write joint acceptance tests for every interface before any work begins. Put every interface decision — not just the deliverable, but the rationale — in writing and require both sides to sign.

**Action.** Run a 30-minute pre-mortem at project kickoff: "If this interface fails, what did each party assume the other would handle?" Publish the answer as a shared ownership register. Revisit it at each major milestone. An interface without a written owner is a failure without an owner.

*(~215 words)*

## Question 3 — Which CQ Capability Comes First?
*(Slide 54 — Q7, asked by Bailey James)*

**Full question:** "If you were leading an engineering team in India working with Japanese partners on the bullet train project, which CQ capability would you prioritize first, and how would you use it to navigate differences in communication, planning, and engineering standards?"

**[DRAFT — awaiting team review]**

**Drive.** The gap between Japan (UAI 92) and India (UAI 40) on uncertainty avoidance is 52 points — which means two teams on the same project may treat a schedule, a specification, and a verbal commitment as fundamentally different kinds of objects (Hofstede et al., 2010). Knowing that is not enough. You need a plan for Monday morning.

**Knowledge.** The four CQ capabilities build on each other: Drive motivates you to prepare, Knowledge tells you what to prepare for, Strategy turns knowledge into a plan, and Action executes it. CQ Knowledge is the prerequisite — without the scores and their behavioral implications, Strategy has nothing to work with. But CQ Strategy is what actually changes outcomes.

**Strategy comes first in practice.** Before any interaction: map who really makes decisions on each side — the authority may not match the org chart (Meyer, 2014). During: treat silence and nodding as signals to follow up privately, not as agreement. After every meeting: send a written recap, invite corrections, and note whether anything surfaces that wasn't said in the room (Heeks et al., 2001; Krishna et al., 2004).

**Action.** Write your decision-rights map before the first planning call. Build a standing 15-minute check-in with the Indian team lead before every group review. Use the corrections to your written recaps as your feedback loop. Knowledge tells you the scores. Strategy tells you what to do on Monday.

*(~250 words)*
