"I walked a team that included engineers in India through a delivery plan. No objections, no questions. I took the silence as agreement and moved on. The team had real concerns. They weren't comfortable raising them in front of the whole group, especially to the person who owned the plan. What I do now: Preview the plan with the local lead first. Ask specific questions. — Aureo Zanon, Presenter

CQ Knowledge What You Need to Know

Meeting Culture

Meetings often confirm a decision rather than debate it. The candid discussion tends to happen before the call, one-on-one, or afterward by email. Takeaway: the meeting is only the visible part of the conversation. Plan for the parts you won't see.

Hierarchy on Technical Teams

Seniority shapes who speaks and when. Junior engineers usually let the senior lead go first. Power Distance: India 77, U.S. 40 (Hofstede et al., 2010). The person who stays quiet in a full-team call is often the one who will tell the team lead their real opinion afterwards. (Krishna et al., 2004; Heeks et al., 2001; Meyer, 2014)

Relationships Before Tasks

Trust is built person to person, and it takes time. Asking about family, a festival, or last night's cricket match is not small talk — it is how cooperation starts. Skipping this step costs credibility you will need later.

Formality

Start formal and let the other person relax it. Expect "Sir," "Ma'am," and emails that open with "Dear." Adding "-ji" to a name (e.g., "Rajesh-ji") shows respect. (slide 10)

Written vs. Verbal Communication

Use both on purpose. Video calls work for status and milestones, but concerns often show up later in writing. Send a short recap after every call and invite corrections explicitly — "Please reply if anything here is wrong or unclear."

Deadlines and Ambiguity

With lower uncertainty avoidance (UAI 40), plans flex, so "yes, it will be done" may mean "we'll do our best." Ask what could make the date slip. India Standard Time runs 10½ hours ahead of U.S. Eastern. Plan around Diwali the way U.S. plants plan around winter shutdowns. (Heeks et al., 2001; Krishna et al., 2004; Hofstede et al., 2010; Mark, personal communication, November 14, 2026)

Five Habits for Your First Meeting

  1. Greet the most senior person first.
  2. Give the first few minutes to people, not the agenda.
  3. Swap "Any questions?" for "What risks do you see?"
  4. A nod or head tilt often means "I'm following," not "I agree."
  5. Close with a written summary, owners, and dates.

Mark's Parallel Case

Same pattern, different company. Mark, who coordinates product development with an India-based team, noticed issues going unmentioned after he delivered bad news. More one-on-ones and bringing local leads in directly improved the flow of information. Silence in a group meeting is information, not consent. (Heeks et al., 2001; Mark, personal communication, November 14, 2026)

Watch out for overgeneralizing. India is regionally diverse. National Hofstede scores describe statistical tendencies across millions of people, not predictions about any individual. Treat these patterns as a starting point for observation, not a template for assumptions.

CQ Strategy Plan Ahead

Meyer's Three-Step Cross-Cultural Change Approach

  1. Pre-brief the senior lead privately 24–48 hours before any group presentation
  2. Reframe your message in locally meaningful terms (relationship, long-term partnership, shared credit)
  3. Identify and empower a trusted internal champion before you need one

(Meyer, 2014)

Boundary-Object Checklist

  • Shared glossary — agree on key terms before work begins
  • Joint sign-off checklist — what does "done" mean to both sides?
  • Overlap-hour standing meeting — synchronous touchpoint, not email-only
  • Written recap after every call with explicit corrections invited

(Krishna et al., 2004)

Apply It — Outsourcing Relationship Scenario

Situation: Six months into a U.S.–India engineering partnership, your Indian team built a module to a spec you thought you'd updated. The documentation was complete on your end. The client is asking for answers.
Step 1 What do you do first?
Risky. Group visibility before a private conversation creates a face-threat. The team lead needs to hear this privately first — public accountability without prior relationship damage trust and reduces information flow. (Meyer, 2014)
Best practice. A private call preserves face, opens problem-solving, and usually surfaces the real issue faster. Mark's experience confirms: more one-on-ones and local lead involvement improved information flow after bad news. (Krishna et al., 2004; Mark, personal communication, November 14, 2026)
Step 2 The team lead explains the spec update was sent by email but was not marked clearly as a change from a prior version. What systemic fix do you propose?
Correct. A joint boundary object — a formal, versioned change log both sides explicitly sign off on — closes the documentation gap. It makes the shared expectation visible and removes ambiguity. (Krishna et al., 2004)
Calls help but do not address the root cause: an unclear documentation standard. Without a joint acceptance artifact, more meetings just surface misalignments that could have been prevented. (Heeks et al., 2001)

Knowledge Checks

1. According to the deck, what does "silence in a group meeting" most likely indicate in an India-based team context?
Verbatim from slide 12: "Silence in a group meeting is information, not consent." With PDI 77, concerns surface one-on-one or afterward in writing — not in front of the person who owns the plan. (Heeks et al., 2001; Mark, personal communication, November 14, 2026)
With PDI 77 and collectivist dynamics, group silence almost always means someone is uncomfortable raising concerns in front of authority — not that they agree or are disinterested. (Heeks et al., 2001)
2. What are "boundary objects" as described by Krishna et al. (2004)?
Krishna et al. (2004) found that successful Western–Indian outsourcing teams developed shared work practices through deliberate boundary-object creation: joint documentation, overlap hours, joint prototyping. These artifacts make both sides' assumptions visible and testable.
Boundary objects are shared work artifacts that cross organizational and cultural boundaries — not tools, contracts, or physical items. (Krishna et al., 2004)
3. India Standard Time is how many hours ahead of U.S. Eastern time?
IST is UTC+5:30, so it runs 10½ hours ahead of U.S. Eastern Standard Time (UTC−5) and 9½ hours ahead during EDT. An 8:00 a.m. EST call starts at 6:30 p.m. in India — plan schedules accordingly and treat Diwali the way U.S. plants treat winter shutdowns. (slide 10)
India Standard Time is UTC+5:30. The U.S. Eastern zone runs UTC−5 (EST) or UTC−4 (EDT), making the gap 10½ or 9½ hours. (slide 10)
