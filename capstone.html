Question 1

"When Indian and Japanese engineers disagree about a design change, who should have final approval, and how can that process preserve both safety and trust?"

Source: peer question, slide 52

Aim for 150–250 words. Touch on CQ Drive, Knowledge, Strategy, and Action.

Model answer

Drive. A design disagreement on a safety-critical system that never gets voiced is a liability, not a process gap. With India's Power Distance at 77, a junior engineer's objection may never reach the room, so silence is not consent (Hofstede et al., 2010).

Knowledge. In high-power-distance settings, objections tend to surface one-on-one after the meeting rather than in front of the group (Meyer, 2014). Mark, who coordinates product development with an India-based team, saw issues go unmentioned after bad news was shared; more one-on-ones and closer involvement of local leads restored the flow of information (Mark, personal communication, November 14, 2026).

Strategy. Agree on who decides what before the first real dispute. Write a decision-rights matrix that names one technical authority per domain, signed off by both sides, and set up a safety stop channel that lets any engineer raise a concern without going through rank.

Action. Collect objections one-on-one before every formal review, and hold a private check-in with the Indian team lead 24–48 hours before sign-off. Put one rule in the project charter: a safety concern overrides seniority at any stage. Decide who decides before you disagree, and let safety outrank seniority.

Sources: Hofstede et al. (2010); Meyer (2014); Mark, personal communication, November 14, 2026.

Question 2

"When it comes to the cooperation and mix of Japanese track design, European signaling, and Indian rolling stock, who would own a failure at those interfaces, and how should the partnership define accountability before the first incident rather than after?"

Source: peer question, slide 53

Aim for 150–250 words. Touch on CQ Drive, Knowledge, Strategy, and Action.

Model answer

Drive. An interface without a named owner is a failure without an owner. Where Japanese track design, European signaling and Indian rolling stock meet is exactly where unspoken assumptions pile up.

Knowledge. Cross-cultural outsourcing partnerships work when both sides actively close gaps in expectations and processes (Heeks et al., 2001). With India's uncertainty avoidance at 40, plans are treated as flexible, so "yes, it will be done" may mean "we'll do our best" rather than a binding guarantee (Hofstede et al., 2010). Shared boundary objects, such as joint documentation, written acceptance criteria and overlapping work hours, turn that ambiguity into engineering accountability (Krishna et al., 2004).

Strategy. Before the first handoff, name one interface owner on each side plus a bridge person who understands both engineering contexts. Write joint acceptance tests for every interface before work begins, and record each interface decision, with its rationale, in a document both sides sign.

Action. Run a 30-minute pre-mortem at kickoff: "If this interface fails, what did each party assume the other would handle?" Publish the answers as a shared ownership register and revisit it at every major milestone.

Sources: Heeks et al. (2001); Hofstede et al. (2010); Krishna et al. (2004).

Question 3

"If you were leading an engineering team in India working with Japanese partners on the bullet train project, which CQ capability would you prioritize first, and how would you use it to navigate differences in communication, planning, and engineering standards?"

Source: peer question, slide 54

Aim for 150–250 words. Touch on CQ Drive, Knowledge, Strategy, and Action.

Model answer

Short answer: CQ Strategy comes first in practice, built on CQ Knowledge.

Drive. Japan's uncertainty avoidance is 92 and India's is 40, a 52-point gap (Hofstede et al., 2010). Two teams on the same project may treat a schedule, a specification and a verbal commitment as different kinds of promises. Knowing that matters only if it changes what you do on Monday.

Knowledge. The four capabilities build on each other: Drive motivates you to prepare, Knowledge tells you what to prepare for, Strategy turns that into a plan, and Action carries it out. Knowledge is the prerequisite, but Strategy is what changes outcomes.

Strategy. Before any interaction, map who really makes decisions on each side; it may not match the org chart (Meyer, 2014). During meetings, treat silence and nodding as a cue to follow up privately, not as agreement. After every meeting, send a written recap and invite corrections (Heeks et al., 2001; Krishna et al., 2004).

Action. Write your decision-rights map before the first planning call, hold a standing 15-minute check-in with the Indian team lead before every group review, and use the corrections to your recaps as your feedback loop. Knowledge tells you the scores; Strategy tells you what to do on Monday.

Sources: Hofstede et al. (2010); Meyer (2014); Heeks et al. (2001); Krishna et al. (2004).
