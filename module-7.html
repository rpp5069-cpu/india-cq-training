Innovation & Diffusion of Innovations in India

Rickey Patel · Slides 35–50

CQ Drive

Why This Matters

Scenario: Your company wants to roll out a new IoT-based quality control system to its India-based manufacturing partners. You have solid U.S. adoption data. Your manager says to send the case study and ask for a go/no-go decision by Friday. You send it Thursday morning. Friday: no response. Monday: "We're still reviewing." Week three: silence. What went wrong?

The adoption silence is not a technical objection — it is a diffusion failure. The proposal went to the group before the authority figure was pre-briefed. The U.S. evidence was used as the main proof point to an audience that needed local evidence. And a one-time go/no-go decision was expected from a culture where adoption unfolds in stages. This module gives you Rogers' framework, the UPI case, and Meyer's champion strategy to fix all three.

CQ Knowledge

Rogers' Diffusion of Innovations

Rogers (2003) adopter categories with 16% critical mass tipping point. First 16% = Innovators (2.5%) + Early Adopters (13.5%). Source: Rogers (2003); Desmarchelier & Fang (2016)

Five Adopter Categories

  • Innovators (2.5%): "Venturesome, cosmopolitan, risk tolerant. They want to be first. High change tolerance — these are your champions."
  • Early Adopters (13.5%): "Respected opinion leaders. They validate the innovation for the mainstream. The tipping point requires you to reach them. 'If [local expert] uses it, it must be good.'"
  • Early Majority (34%): "Deliberate. They need social proof from local peers, not foreign case studies. They wait to see that it works."
  • Late Majority (34%): "Skeptical. They adopt when most of their peers already have — out of social pressure, not conviction."
  • Laggards (16%): "Tradition-bound. They adopt last, if ever."

The 16% rule: The tipping point (innovators + early adopters) is when adoption becomes self-sustaining. Before that point, every adoption requires active effort. After it, the mainstream starts moving on its own. (Rogers, 2003; Desmarchelier & Fang, 2016)

Five Innovation Attributes

Rogers five innovation attributes and India deployment implications
Attribute Definition India Deployment Implication
Relative advantage Does it do better than the alternative? Frame in productivity terms: cost per unit, downtime reduction. Local benchmarks beat U.S. comparisons.
Compatibility Does it fit existing norms and infrastructure? Power reliability, operator fluency, local integration with existing software/hardware
Complexity How hard is it to understand and use? In-language training materials; operator certification; complexity raises inhibitor risk
Trialability Can they try it before committing? Pilot programs with no-penalty exit clauses lower early-majority risk aversion
Observability Can they see it working for someone similar? Local success stories, video of a similar farm or factory, in-person site visits to local adopters

Source: (Rogers, 2003)

UPI as a Diffusion Case Study

UPI transaction volume FY2018–FY2024. Source: National Payments Corporation of India (2024); Ministry of Finance (2024)

UPI through Rogers' Lens

  • Relative advantage: replaced cash and NEFT/RTGS; zero merchant fee initially (National Payments Corporation of India, 2024)
  • Compatibility: designed for India's mobile-first population; Jan Dhan mandate extended banking access to 500M+ unbanked citizens (Ministry of Finance, 2024)
  • Complexity: single phone number = payment address; works on feature phones via USSD (*99#)
  • Trialability: mobile wallets allowed trial before full commitment
  • Observability: 0.92B → 131.1B transactions FY2018–FY2024 — the visible scale proved adoption to skeptics (National Payments Corporation of India, 2024)

The Jan Dhan–Aadhaar–Mobile (JAM) infrastructure stack was the prerequisite that made UPI's diffusion possible. (Nilekani & Shah, 2015)

Watch out for overgeneralizing. India is regionally diverse. National Hofstede scores describe statistical tendencies across millions of people, not predictions about any individual. Treat these patterns as a starting point for observation, not a template for assumptions.

Meyer's Three-Step Champion Framework

When you need to gain adoption in a high-PDI, collectivist culture, go in three steps — not one big presentation. (Meyer, 2014)

Step 1 — Private Pre-Brief

Before any group presentation or formal proposal: meet privately with the most senior decision-maker (or their trusted deputy) 24–48 hours in advance. Share the idea informally. Ask for their perspective. Listen for concerns you can address before the room hears them. Objective: get the authority figure into the room with context, not surprise.

Step 2 — Reframe as a Local Win

Position the innovation in terms of local advantage, not foreign case studies. What local problem does it solve? What local capability does it demonstrate? What credit does the local team get? In collectivist, high-PDI culture, "this will make our team look good" is a stronger motivator than "this worked in another country." (Meyer, 2014)

Step 3 — Identify and Empower a Local Champion

Find a trusted local peer (Early Adopter) who can vouch for the innovation in front of the Early Majority. The champion should be credible to their colleagues — not a foreign advocate, but someone the team trusts. Give them a named role in the pilot: they're not a validator, they're a co-owner. (Rogers, 2003; Meyer, 2014)

Dearing & Kreuter Mistakes Applied to India

Fix (Do) Mistake (Avoid) Why
Mistake #1 fix: Collect local performance data before the launch meeting — even a small pilot with comparable local conditions Presenting U.S. adoption data as the main proof point to an India-based team Local evidence is orders of magnitude more persuasive to Early Majority adopters than foreign case studies, especially in a high-PDI culture where local authority vouches for local evidence (Dearing & Kreuter, 2010)
Mistake #4 fix: Identify the local opinion leader (the respected technical expert whose adoption signals legitimacy) and brief them first Launching to a broad group before the local authority figure has heard and endorsed the idea In Rogers' framework, Early Adopter opinion leaders pull the Early Majority into adoption. In India's high-PDI culture, the authority chain amplifies this — skipping the leader breaks the diffusion pathway (Rogers, 2003; Dearing & Kreuter, 2010)
Mistake #7 fix: Design a multi-stage adoption plan: trial → verify → expand → document → reference Expecting a single presentation to produce a final go/no-go decision In low-UAI India, plans flex — but high PDI means decisions require multiple levels of sign-off. Adoption is a process that unfolds over time, not a moment (Dearing & Kreuter, 2010; Hofstede et al., 2010)
Apply It

IoT Deployment Scenario

You are deploying an IoT quality control system to a 400-person manufacturing plant in Pune. You have strong U.S. data. Week 1: you send a proposal packet. Week 3: still "under review."

1 What was the first mistake?
Correct. This is Dearing & Kreuter Mistake #4 (underestimating opinion leaders) combined with Meyer Step 1 failure. In a high-PDI culture, proposals that land in a group inbox without prior authority pre-brief feel like surprises — and surprises get delayed. (Dearing & Kreuter, 2010; Meyer, 2014)
More detail isn't the problem — the problem is sequence. The authority figure needs to hear this privately before the team does. Technical depth matters, but only after the relationship channel is open. (Meyer, 2014)
The deadline isn't the issue — the silence is. And silence in a high-PDI culture after an unsolicited group proposal means the right people weren't pre-briefed. The "Friday by end of week" expectation is a low-PDI assumption. (Hofstede et al., 2010)
2 You get a 30-minute call with the plant manager. How do you open it?
Incorrect. Opening with ROI before relationship is the classic Dearing & Kreuter Mistake #1 failure (over-relying on evidence from other settings). The manager needs to feel heard before they evaluate your proposal. Open with their context, not your data. (Dearing & Kreuter, 2010)
Correct. This is CQ Strategy in action: gather local context before presenting your solution. Meyer Step 2 says to reframe as a local win — but you can't do that without first understanding what the local challenges are. (Meyer, 2014; Rogers, 2003)
3 The manager is interested but says "we'd want to see it work somewhere similar first." What do you do?
Incorrect. This is Dearing & Kreuter Mistake #1: over-relying on evidence from other settings. The manager explicitly asked for a local analog. U.S. testimonials don't satisfy that need — a local pilot does. (Dearing & Kreuter, 2010)
Correct. This addresses Trialability (Rogers' 5 attributes), gives the manager's team local evidence to show their peers (Observability), and positions the team as co-owners (Meyer Step 3). The "no-penalty exit" reduces Early Majority risk aversion. (Rogers, 2003; Meyer, 2014)

Test Your Understanding

1 Rogers' (2003) 16% tipping point in diffusion refers to:
Correct. The 16% tipping point is when Innovators (2.5%) + Early Adopters (13.5%) together reach critical mass, and the Early Majority begins to adopt based on social proof from their peers rather than waiting for external push. Before 16%, every adoption requires active effort. (Rogers, 2003; Desmarchelier & Fang, 2016)
The 16% tipping point is a diffusion dynamics concept, not a profitability or success metric. It is the point where adoption becomes self-sustaining because enough opinion leaders have adopted that the Early Majority begins moving on peer evidence. (Rogers, 2003)
2 Dearing & Kreuter's (2010) Mistake #1 — over-relying on evidence from other settings — is especially problematic in India because:
Correct. In high-PDI, collectivist cultures, local authority figures are the most credible information source for the Early Majority — far more than foreign case studies. A respected local expert's endorsement is worth more than a stack of U.S. ROI reports. (Dearing & Kreuter, 2010; Rogers, 2003; Hofstede et al., 2010)
The issue is source credibility. In high-PDI cultures, local authority figures carry more persuasive weight than foreign evidence. A local pilot or local opinion leader endorsement addresses the same risk concern more effectively. (Dearing & Kreuter, 2010)
3 UPI's growth from 0.92B to 131.1B transactions (FY2018–FY2024) illustrates which Rogers attribute most directly?
All five attributes contributed to UPI's diffusion, but Observability is most directly illustrated by the growth curve itself: the visible scale (0.92B → 131.1B transactions) proved to skeptics that adoption was real and widespread, pulling in the Early and Late Majority through social proof. (National Payments Corporation of India, 2024; Rogers, 2003)
While Relative Advantage and Trialability were also important, the growth curve from 0.92B to 131.1B most directly illustrates Observability — the visible proof of adoption at scale that creates social proof for skeptics. (Rogers, 2003; National Payments Corporation of India, 2024)
