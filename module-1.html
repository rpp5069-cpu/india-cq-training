Module 1 — Country & Cultural Dimensions Overview

Caroline Auer · Slides 3–7

CQ Drive Why This Matters

Opening scenario: You have just been assigned as the U.S.-side lead on a joint engineering project with a team based in Pune. Week one: you email your Indian counterpart asking for a direct yes or no on a spec change. No reply for three days. You send a follow-up. Silence again. What is happening, and how should you respond?

This module gives you the cultural framework to answer that question — and to prevent it from happening in the first place.

CQ Knowledge What You Need to Know

Country Snapshot

  • India is the 7th largest country by area and borders six countries: Pakistan, China, Nepal, Bhutan, Bangladesh, and Myanmar.
  • World's 2nd most populated country. Capital: New Delhi. Official languages: English and Hindi. 22 scheduled languages in total.
  • Hinduism is the dominant religion; large Muslim population. Culture is highly regionalized — the difference between Mumbai, Chennai, Kolkata, and Pune is significant.
  • Spirituality, community, food, and hospitality are central values across regions.
  • Key historical events that shape organizational culture today: Mughal Empire, British colonial rule and Independence (1947), ongoing border disputes and national identity.

Watch out for overgeneralizing. India is regionally diverse. National Hofstede scores describe statistical tendencies across millions of people, not predictions about any individual. Treat these patterns as a starting point for observation, not a template for assumptions.

Hofstede Explorer — India's Cultural Profile

PDI Power Distance
77

High power distance — Authority figures decide who speaks and when. Chain of command is strictly followed. Decisions require senior sign-off. Technical expertise does carry weight, but approval flows top-down. Silence from a subordinate often signals discomfort, not agreement. (Hofstede et al., 2010; Juhász, 2014)

IDV Individualism
48

Moderately collectivist — In-group loyalty matters. Credit for work is shared with the team, not claimed individually. Relationships with the in-group are deep; out-group relationships are more transactional. Individual initiative is valued within the group, not at the group's expense. (Hofstede et al., 2010)

MAS Masculinity
56

Moderately masculine — Achievement, competition, and advancement matter — but so does team welfare and harmony. Recognition for contributions is important; public embarrassment is damaging. (Hofstede et al., 2010)

UAI Uncertainty Avoidance
40

Low uncertainty avoidance — Ambiguity and changing requirements are tolerated, even embraced (Jugaad mindset). "Yes, it will be done" may mean "we'll do our best." Ask what could make the date slip. Plans flex. (Hofstede et al., 2010)

LTO Long-Term Orientation
51

Balanced long-term orientation — Respects tradition but also pragmatic about adapting. Relationship investment pays off over time. Quick wins are possible, but deep partnerships require patience. (Hofstede et al., 2010)

IVR Indulgence
26

Restrained — Work obligations take priority over personal leisure in professional settings. Formal norms govern workplace expression. Gratification is controlled. (Hofstede et al., 2010)

CQ Strategy Plan Ahead

High-PDI Pre-Meeting Checklist

  1. Who are the decision-makers? (The person who speaks may not be the one who decides.)
  2. Have senior sponsors been briefed privately before this meeting?
  3. Is silence from a team member agreement, or is it face-saving avoidance?
  4. Have I given the most senior person a chance to speak first?
  5. Am I prepared to follow up one-on-one after the group call?

CQ Action Do This, Not That

Do Avoid Why
Address senior engineers by title (Dr./Mr./Ms./Eng.) until invited to use first names First-name-only email introductions without relationship groundwork PDI 77 — titles signal respect and position you as a credible counterpart (Hofstede et al., 2010)
Copy the most senior person on emails, even informational updates Flat org-chart assumptions — skipping hierarchy in communications Hierarchy drives information flow; exclusion can stall decisions and signal disrespect
Open meetings with 2–3 minutes of personal check-in before the agenda Starting directly with the deliverable or action items Trust precedes commitment in collectivist, high-PDI cultures (AtoZ International Business, n.d.)

Apply It — Branching Scenario

Situation: Your Indian team lead has gone quiet after you sent a design-review request. It's been four days. You have a client deadline in two weeks.
Decision 1 What do you do first?
Incorrect. Escalating before relationship-building signals distrust and may publicly embarrass the team lead. In high-PDI cultures, going above someone's head without warning damages the relationship and can freeze communication entirely. (Hofstede et al., 2010)
Correct. A private, low-pressure conversation preserves face and opens dialogue. High PDI cultures often communicate disagreement indirectly or in private — your silence is their discomfort, not agreement. (Juhász, 2014; Meyer, 2014)
Partially useful but misses the core issue. More information doesn't address the face-saving dynamic. The team lead needs a private channel to raise concerns — a detailed email adds pressure without providing one. (Heeks et al., 2001)

Knowledge Checks

1. India's PDI score of 77 most directly predicts which behavior in an engineering team?
PDI 77 means authority is concentrated at the top. Junior engineers wait for the senior lead to speak first; decisions flow top-down; objections surface privately. (Hofstede et al., 2010)
PDI measures hierarchy and power concentration. India's score of 77 (vs. U.S. 40) means seniority strongly shapes who speaks, decides, and signs off. (Hofstede et al., 2010)
2. India's low UAI score of 40 is most likely to produce which behavior?
Low UAI means ambiguity is tolerated and even embraced — the Jugaad innovation mindset (frugal, flexible, good-enough solutions) is a direct cultural expression of low uncertainty avoidance. (Hofstede et al., 2010)
Low UAI does not mean slow or bureaucratic — that would be high UAI. India's 40 means teams adapt quickly, timelines flex, and "yes, we'll try" may mean exactly that. (Hofstede et al., 2010)
3. A U.S. engineer nods at every statement your Indian counterpart makes in a first meeting. What should you assume?
A nod or head tilt in Indian communication often signals "I am following you," not "I agree." Treat nods as engagement indicators, not consent signals. Follow up with specific questions: "What risks do you see?" rather than "Any questions?" (Meyer, 2014)
Be careful not to interpret non-verbal cues through a U.S. lens. The nodding pattern in Indian communication is culturally distinct and does not directly map to agreement. (Meyer, 2014)
4. Which of the following best describes the relationship between Hinduism and Power Distance in India?
Speaker notes from the deck (slide 6) note: "Strong correlation between a country's religion and several respective Hofstede scores. The Hofstede dimension that correlates most with the Hindu religion is Power Distance." This is consistent with the PDI 77 score. (Hofstede et al., 2010)
The deck explicitly notes a correlation between Hinduism and high Power Distance in India's organizational context. (Hofstede et al., 2010)
